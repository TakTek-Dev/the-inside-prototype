The Long Read · Knesset election 2026 18days to polling day, Tuesday 27 October

Two lists, one ruling, and the question that decides the rest: will the Arab vote turn out?

A week ago both Arab-led lists were struck off the ballot. The Supreme Court put them back, but not everyone on them. With polls giving the Joint List and Ra’am between 12 and 15 seats together, the arithmetic of Israel’s next government may run through towns from the Galilee to the Naqab.

The Inside desk
Six chapters · about 14 minutes

On 23 September, the Central Elections Committee voted to bar both the Joint List and Ra’am from the 27 October election, together with Hadash MK Ofer Cassif and Balad chairman Sami Abu Shehadeh. For nine days, the parties that have spoken for most Palestinian citizens of Israel in the Knesset were, on paper, not running at all.

On 2 October the Supreme Court unanimously reversed the bans on both lists, and by seven votes to two restored Cassif. Abu Shehadeh was a different case. The court’s president, Yitzhak Amit, told him a majority of the nine justices favoured disqualification, and he was given until Friday to withdraw. He did.

So the ballot is set: a Joint List of Hadash, Ta’al and Balad, headed by Hadash chairman Yousef Jabareen, and a Ra’am running alone, with a Jewish former Yesh Atid MK, Yoav Segalovitz, on its slate. The two have signed a surplus-vote agreement. What neither can sign for is the voter who stays home.

In November 2022, turnout among Arab citizens was 53.2 per cent. A Moshe Dayan Center survey published in May projected about the same again. Everything in this read comes back to that number.

I Chapter one

Struck off, written back

The committee’s vote on 23 September was not a surprise in form. Israel’s election panel, made up of party representatives, has voted to bar Arab candidates before, and the Supreme Court has almost always overruled it.

What was new was the scale: both Arab-led lists at once, weeks before polling day. Jabareen asked how a committee could bar parties representing more than 1.25 million voters. The legal centre Adalah said it would challenge the decision, and the two lists appealed to the Supreme Court on 29 September.

The ruling on 2 October found that the committee’s decision did not meet the legal requirements for exclusion. The same week, petitions to disqualify the parties of Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich were rejected by the committee itself.

II Chapter two

The man who stepped aside

Sami Abu Shehadeh, Balad’s chairman, was the one name the court would not restore. The push to bar him, led by National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, turned on an article he wrote after 7 October 2023. He denies supporting the attack.

At a hearing on Thursday 1 October, he was told a majority of the bench favoured disqualification and was given until the next day to withdraw. He withdrew, telling Al Jazeera he “didn’t have any other choices”. The attorney general, Gali Baharav-Miara, had backed his disqualification while finding no sufficient basis to exclude the far-right parties.

He remains Balad’s leader, and was on stage in Tel Aviv four days later. Balad’s candidates run on the Joint List without him.

III Chapter three

A campaign in Hebrew

Three days after the ruling, the Joint List opened a Hebrew-language campaign in Tel Aviv. Jabareen told the Times of Israel the list expects between 10,000 and 15,000 Jewish votes.

A party source set the bar higher: about one seat’s worth, which on the last election’s figures is just over 36,000 votes. Ahmad Tibi pitched the list as “the address” for left-wing Jews who oppose the war. The real test, though, is in the Triangle, the Galilee and the Naqab.

IV Chapter four

Ra’am’s other road

Mansour Abbas stayed out of the Joint List for the same reason he took Ra’am into a coalition in 2021: he wants a seat at the table, not a bloc outside it.

The frame was set months ago. At Ra’am’s campaign launch in Rahat in June, held with the southern branch of the Islamic Movement, Abbas called the election a “war of existence” and set a goal of lifting the party’s Naqab vote from about 80,000 to 100,000. In late August he added Yoav Segalovitz, a Jewish former police official and Yesh Atid MK, to his slate, a pairing built around fighting crime in Arab towns.

V Chapter five

The turnout question

The Dayan Center’s May survey, for its Konrad Adenauer Program for Jewish-Arab Cooperation, found Arab turnout would be 53 per cent if the election were held then, about the 53.2 per cent of November 2022.

It also modelled a reunited list of all four parties, Ra’am included, and found that would lift turnout to 67 per cent, the highest the series has recorded. That list was not formed. The campaign now runs on two lists, a ruling that cut one leader from the ballot, and a war that the Israel Democracy Institute found weighs differently by community: in its September survey, 32 per cent of Arab respondents called 7 October central to their vote, against 68 per cent of Jewish respondents.

VI Chapter six

After the count

Three of the four polls published on 5–8 October leave the parties of Netanyahu’s current coalition short of 61 seats; Channel 14’s gives them 63. That is why the Arab-led lists matter after the count, and why both camps are arguing about them now.

On 26 September Gadi Eisenkot, Naftali Bennett, Yair Lapid, Avigdor Liberman and Yair Golan signed a pact to build a government together. Likud’s reply was that there is no Eisenkot government without Ra’am. Bennett has said the Arab parties “are not Zionist, and therefore we will not rely on them”. Eisenkot’s conditions for any partner leave room for Ra’am alone, and he has stopped short of ruling out Arab support. In the Dayan Center’s survey, 77.2 per cent of Arab respondents said an Arab party should join the next government.
